Dictionaries define words by other words, so a dictionary can teach new words only to a reader who already knows part of its vocabulary. Representing a dictionary as a directed graph, in which each word points to the headwords in whose definitions it occurs, makes this part measurable (Vincent-Lamarre et al., 2016). The Kernel is the part from which all other headwords can be defined; a minimal grounding set (MinSet) is a smallest set of words from which every other headword can be learned through definitions alone.

We analyse this graph for the second edition of the DEX (Dicționarul explicativ al limbii române), digitised by dexonline: 63,843 headwords and 600,473 definitional links. Because Romanian is richly inflected, many word forms in definitions are ambiguous (are: avea 'have' or ara 'plough'). A large language model assigns each to a headword; all results are repeated without the model, and integer linear programming bounds the MinSet.

The Kernel of the DEX contains 33.2% of the headwords and the MinSet 9.7–9.9%. Processed in the same way, dictionaries of eleven other languages have MinSets of 0.6–3.1%. The difference comes mainly from reciprocal definition, five to twenty times more frequent in the DEX: 8,761 pairs of headwords occur in each other's definitions (arbore–copac, both 'tree'). Although derived only from definitions, the Kernel contains 98% of the Romanian equivalents of the Swadesh list, a standard list of about 200 basic meanings used in comparative linguistics, and 94.5% of the 1,419 words that Graur (1954) proposed as the fundamental lexical stock of Romanian, against 33.2% expected by chance. The words most used in definitions, in contrast, are mainly learned French loanwords. Dictionary definitions thus preserve a linguistically meaningful lexical core, and the graph gives lexicographers a map of circularity. Data and code are public.
