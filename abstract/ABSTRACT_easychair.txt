Dictionaries define words by other words, so every dictionary is circular: a reader who knows none of the defining words can learn nothing from it. Vincent-Lamarre et al. (2016) measured this circularity in English dictionaries by representing a dictionary as a directed graph in which each word is linked to the headwords in whose definitions it occurs. They computed two sets: the Kernel, the part of the dictionary from which all other headwords can be defined (7–12% of headwords), and the minimal grounding set (MinSet), the smallest set of words from which every other headword can be learned through definitions (about 1%).

We apply the method for the first time to Romanian, to the second edition of the DEX (Dicționarul explicativ al limbii române) digitised by dexonline (63,843 headwords). Many word forms in definitions are ambiguous (are: avea 'have' or ara 'plough'). A large language model assigns each such form to a headword; on a random sample it agrees with a stronger model on 87% of content-word decisions, and all results are repeated with two procedures that use no model.

The Kernel of the DEX contains 33.2% of the headwords and the MinSet 9.7–9.9%, three to five and about ten times, respectively, the proportions found in English. The main cause is reciprocal definition: 8,761 pairs of headwords occur in each other's definitions (arbore–copac, both 'tree'). The Kernel nevertheless contains the basic vocabulary: 98% of the Romanian equivalents of the Swadesh list, a standard list of about 200 basic meanings used in comparative linguistics, and 94.5% of the 1,419 words that Graur (1954) proposed as the fundamental lexical stock of Romanian, against 33.2% expected by chance. The words used most often in definitions, by contrast, are mainly learned French loanwords. The data and code are publicly available.
